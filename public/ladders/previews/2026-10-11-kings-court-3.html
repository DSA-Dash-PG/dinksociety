King's Court #3 just got bigger. A fifth court went in, the field grew to 20, and the waitlist emptied out. That changes the math: King Court is Court A, and from Court E it now takes four straight wins to get there. And this one is DUPR rated, so every rally follows you home.

The field is a strange, wonderful mix. Two King's Court winners. A guy with 19 ladder nights. A guy with no DUPR rating who keeps winning anyway. And seven men walking in with no King's Court history at all.

Seventeen of the twenty are rated, averaging 3.53, with nearly two full points between the top (4.350) and the bottom (2.416). But not every number carries the same weight. DUPR's reliability score says how much match data sits behind a rating.

Ten men are at 70% or better. Ryan, Eli, Kai, Richard and Stan sit at 100%; Robert, Chandler and Yoshi at 80%; Matt and Guilbert at 70%. Those numbers are earned. Five are at 20% or below: Terrance and Francis at 10%, John and Teddie at 20%, and Nguyen at 1%. A 3.3 at 100% is a fact. A 3.5 at 1% is a rumor.

The throne

Ryan Hom is the defending King, and his 4.128 is the best rating in the building with 100% reliability behind it. His King's Court #2 went like this: dropped his opener 7–11 on the bottom court, then won seven straight, reached King Court by Round 4 and never left. Across two King's Courts he's 13–3 at +75. He also went 10–0 at the August DUPR ladder. The book on beating him is short and mostly blank pages.

A reminder for the King's Court first-timers: the King isn't whoever parks on Court A. It's the man with the most wins, then point differential, then DR. Court A is where you want to be at the end of the night, and the ladder usually sorts itself out so the best players log the most games there. The fifth court adds a wrinkle: one more rung means one more round for whoever starts low, and more rounds against the best teams for whoever climbs. Every one of those is a loss waiting to happen.

The contenders

Jason Phan is the most interesting name on the sheet. He has no DUPR rating. He also won King's Court #1 at 7–1, +34. At King's Court #2 he spent five rounds on King Court and came out 4–4 — call it the King Court tax: you only play the best teams, so even a great night looks ordinary. Sunday he walks out with his first DUPR number. Nobody would be shocked if it starts with a 4.

Kai Pylkkanen logged six rounds on King Court at #2, the most of anyone in that field, and finished second at 6–2. He's a two-time ladder winner going back to June and carries a 3.754 at 100% reliability. If anyone can match Ryan win for win, it's him.

Eli Henry has the third-highest DUPR in the field at 3.910, fully reliable, and went 5–3 at #2 — but spent only one round on King Court, bouncing between the middle courts most of the night. That's a guy winning on the wrong courts. He's coming in warm, too: second at Gundo Friyay on Thursday, 6–4 at +21. If the draw is kind early, he's a podium threat.

Chandler Hong is back, and the last time he played a King's Court he lived up top: six rounds on King Court at #1 on the way to a 5–3 fourth place. He also went 6–4 at Gundo Friyay. His 3.550 is 80% reliable, so what you see is roughly what you get.

Matt Pasqualetto might be the streakiest player here. King's Court #1: third place, 6–2, six rounds on King Court. King's Court #2: seventh, 4–4, −11. With five ladder podiums on his record, the KC1 version is the real one — the question is which one shows up at 11am.

The middle class (and the movers)

Erick Li went exactly .500 at King's Court #1 with a +1 differential, which is the pickleball equivalent of breaking even at the casino and calling it a good night. He skipped #2. His 3.649 matches Matt's to the thousandth — but Matt's is 70% reliable and Erick's is 30%. Sunday is when Erick's number finds out what it really is.

Yoshi Nogimura finished King's Court #1 at 4–4 and 57–57. Not one point in either direction. That's either perfect balance or a cry for help. His 3.070 is a well-documented one, at 80% reliability, and he's spent Season 2 winning games that number says he shouldn't — which tells you exactly how much DUPR knows about Yoshi.

Guilbert Balmaceda is a quiet 9–9 across two ladder nights, and at King's Court #1 he went 3–5 while still outscoring the field 62–60. His 3.320 is 70% reliable. A couple of bounces his way and he's in the top half.

Peter Phan is the other hard-luck story. He outscored his opponents 70–68 at King's Court #2 and still went 3–5. He's also unrated on DUPR, so Sunday hands him his first number.

Teddie Liu went 1–7 at King's Court #1, and he's back anyway. Respect. His 2.416 is only 20% reliable, so a few good rounds could move it a long way, and on a five-court ladder there are plenty of rounds to find.

And the biggest mover between the first two King's Courts: Richard Hak, 10th to 5th. With 19 ladder nights, he's played more ladders than anyone in the Society — the floor is high, and so is the mileage.

New blood

Dhruv Ahluwalia is the name nobody saw coming: a 4.350 DUPR, the highest in the field and 0.22 above the defending King. The asterisk is reliability — 50%, so that number rests on about half the data behind Ryan's. Sunday is the audit. If it holds, the throne has a real challenger on his very first King's Court.

Robert Contaoi walks in at 3.804 with 80% reliability, which makes him the most trustworthy rating among the debutants and the fourth-highest in the building. No Dink Society ladder history, so nobody here has a book on him yet.

Stan Esperon makes his King's Court debut, but he's no rookie. Nine ladder nights, two ladder wins — Friday Night Lights on May 8 (6–1, +60) and the Thursday Night Challenger on July 2 (8–2, +45). When Stan wins a ladder, he doesn't win it by a little.

Terrance Ford (3.550, 10%), John Ito (3.425, 20%) and Francis Castillo (3.017, 10%) are also debuting, and all three ratings are thin — a few rated games could swing any of them. Francis has one Dink Society ladder on the books, a 5–4 Saturday morning back in June.

Sumeer Ahluwalia is on DUPR but unrated, so like Jason and Peter, he leaves Sunday with a number.

Nguyen Vu shows a 3.512 — at 1% reliability, which is DUPR's way of saying "we've barely met." Treat it as a placeholder until the scores come in.

Welcome, gentlemen. Bring water. Court E builds character. Court A is where you want to be when the night ends.

Who's missing

One regular from the first two King's Courts isn't in this field: Kevin Phan, third at #2 (5–3, +15). That leaves room behind Ryan for a first-time finisher in the top three.

Five things to watch

  1. Wins are the crown, not the court. The King is the most wins, then point differential, then DR. Living on Court A means facing the best teams every round, so a 6–2 night lower down beats a 5–3 night up top. Ask Jason Phan: five rounds on King Court at #2, and a 4–4 to show for it.
  2. Four wins to the top. With five courts, anyone who starts on Court E needs a near-perfect first half just to see Court A.
  3. The Dhruv question. A 4.350 at 50% reliability, on his first King's Court. Either the King has company, or DUPR is about to issue a correction.
  4. Three unrated men. Jason Phan, Sumeer Ahluwalia and Peter Phan get their first DUPR numbers Sunday. Jason has already beaten this crowd once.
  5. Can anyone take a game off the King? Ryan has lost three ladder games total across two King's Courts. Kai, Eli, Jason, Dhruv — and now Robert — are the likeliest to change that.
